What an AI workflow actually does

“AI workflow” sounds bigger than it is. In practice it’s a set of steps that runs on its own when something happens, with AI handling the parts that involve reading or writing language. Everything else is ordinary automation, and a person stays in charge of the decisions that matter.

The plain definition

Every workflow has three parts:

  • A trigger: something happens. A form is submitted, an email arrives, it’s Monday at 8 am.
  • Steps: things that happen as a result. Save the details, check a rule, send a message, update a sheet.
  • An outcome: the work is done, or it’s waiting for a person to approve it.

Most of those steps are plain automation: moving information from one tool to another, following rules you set. The AI part is usually one or two steps where something has to be read, understood or written, such as working out what an email is asking for, or drafting a reply.

An example: a new website enquiry

Here’s how a typical enquiry workflow could run for a small business. This is an example of the kind of workflow we build, not a specific client’s setup.

  1. A customer fills in the contact form on your website. That’s the trigger.
  2. The details are saved to your CRM or a Google Sheet automatically. Nobody copies anything.
  3. AI reads the message and sorts it: a price question, a booking request, a complaint or something else.
  4. AI drafts a reply using your own FAQs, prices and policies, not general internet knowledge.
  5. You approve the reply with one tap, or edit it first. Nothing goes out without a person seeing it.
  6. A follow-up is scheduled. If the customer doesn’t reply in two days, a polite reminder goes out in the same thread.
  7. You get a short summary at the end of the day: new enquiries, replies sent, anything that needs you.

The result: every enquiry gets a quick, accurate reply, nothing is forgotten on a busy day, and you still decide what’s said in your name.

What the AI part is good at

  • Reading messy, free-text messages and working out what they’re about.
  • Pulling details out of emails, PDFs and invoices: names, dates, amounts.
  • Drafting replies in your tone, from your own information.
  • Summarising long threads, call notes or a week of activity.

And what plain automation does better

  • Moving data between tools exactly as it is.
  • Running things on a schedule.
  • Following clear rules: “if the order is over a certain value, notify the owner”.

A good workflow uses AI only where it adds something. Using it for steps that a simple rule can handle just adds cost and room for error.

What it doesn’t do

An AI workflow doesn’t replace your judgement. It shouldn’t decide prices, make promises, approve refunds or handle anything sensitive on its own. Language models can also misread an unusual message or state something wrong with confidence.

That’s why sensible workflows include approvals for anything that commits your business, answers grounded in your own documents, and a log of what each step did, so you can always check.

Good first workflows for a small business

  • Replying to website and WhatsApp enquiries, with you approving the drafts.
  • Following up leads who haven’t replied.
  • Reading invoices or order emails and entering the details into a sheet or accounting tool.
  • Sending appointment reminders and confirmations.
  • A weekly summary of enquiries, sales and anything overdue.

How to tell if a task is worth automating

Look for tasks that tick most of these boxes:

  • It happens every day or every week.
  • It follows roughly the same steps each time.
  • It mostly means reading, copying or sending information.
  • Delays cost you something: a slow reply, a missed follow-up, a late report.
  • The information is already digital, in email, forms or spreadsheets.

If a task needs real expertise on every single case, it may be better left with a person, or handled by AI that drafts and a person who decides.

What you need to get started

  1. A list of the tools you already use: email, WhatsApp, forms, CRM, spreadsheets, accounting.
  2. One process that eats up time, described step by step as it happens today.
  3. A few real examples: past enquiries, replies you were happy with, your FAQs and prices.
  4. A decision on who approves what.

Start with one workflow, run it with approvals switched on, and expand once you trust it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to change the tools I use?

Usually not. Most workflows connect the tools you already have, such as Gmail, WhatsApp Business, Google Sheets, a CRM or accounting software, as long as those tools allow connections.

Is my customer data safe?

It depends on the tools involved, which is why we decide that together at the start. Good practice is to connect only what a workflow needs, keep sensitive steps behind a person’s approval, and keep a log of everything the workflow does.

What happens if the AI gets something wrong?

That’s what approvals and logs are for. Drafts wait for a person before anything is sent, and if a step fails, the workflow stops and tells someone instead of carrying on.
